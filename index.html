📚 About MiniGoogle '98 & PageRank History

The Dawn of Modern Search (1998)

In 1996–1998, Ph.D. students Larry Page and Sergey Brin at Stanford University started a research project called BackRub. At that time, search engines like AltaVista, Lycos, and Excite ranked web pages primarily by counting how many times query keywords appeared in the text.

This keyword-centric approach had a fatal vulnerability: Keyword Stuffing. Unscrupulous webmasters could repeat words like "pizza pizza pizza" hundreds of times in white text on a white background, hijacking top rankings without providing any real value.

Larry Page realized that hyperlinks are citations: when Page A links to Page B, it is casting a vote of confidence. Moreover, votes from important pages carry more weight than votes from obscure pages.

This mathematical breakthrough—formalized as the PageRank algorithm—served as the foundation for Google when it incorporated in September 1998 in a Menlo Park garage.

💾 1998 Search Engine Historical Archive

Search Engines of 1998

Before Google dominated search, the World Wide Web was navigated through a diverse constellation of early portals and crawlers:

  • AltaVista (1995): The speed champion built by Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC). Indexed the full text of millions of pages, but suffered heavily from spam.
  • Yahoo! Directory (1994): A human-edited hierarchical catalog maintained by "surfers" who reviewed submissions manually.
  • Lycos (1994): Originated at Carnegie Mellon University; pioneered document prefix matching and term weighting.
  • Excite (1995): Offered "concept-based searching" using relational statistical models.
  • Ask Jeeves (1997): Promoted natural language question answering with a butler persona.

Fun 1998 Fact: Google's first production server rack was famously built out of Lego bricks in Stanford's Gates Computer Science building because it was cheap, modular, and easy to expand!
